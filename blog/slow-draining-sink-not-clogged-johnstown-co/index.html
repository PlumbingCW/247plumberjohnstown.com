Slow Draining Sink Not Clogged in Johnstown, CO

No standing clog, no gurgle you can fix with a plunger, and still the water crawls. This guide works through the causes in the order a plumber would.

By the Johnstown 24/7 Plumbing team · Published 2026-10-13 · Updated 2026-10-13

Slow is a symptom, not a diagnosis

A sink that drains slowly but never fully plugs is a common call. People try a plunger, a drain stick and a kettle of hot water, and nothing changes. That usually means the problem is not one solid plug sitting in the trap.

Think of the sink as a small system. Water leaves through the strainer, passes the tailpiece and trap, travels along a branch line, and needs air from a vent to keep moving. A fault at any point makes the whole thing slow.

Narrow it down in five checks

Work through these in order before you spend money on products.

  1. Test other drains.

    Run the nearby tub and toilet. If they are slow too, the trouble is farther down the line than this sink.

  2. Fill and release.

    Fill the basin and watch. Fast at first, then a slow tail suggests partial blockage deeper in the line.

  3. Listen for gurgles.

    Gurgling points at the vent or a restricted line pulling air through the trap.

  4. Check the disposal and dishwasher.

    A disposal with a clogged baffle or a dishwasher sharing the line can drag a kitchen sink down.

  5. Pull the trap.

    Place a bucket underneath and open the trap. A clean trap with a slow sink means the problem is past it.

Write down what you find. A plumber who knows the answer to those five checks can bring the right tool on the first visit.

Venting: the cause you cannot see

Drains rely on a vent pipe, usually through the roof, to let air in behind the water. When that vent is blocked, the water has to fight a vacuum, and a sink empties in slow gulps with gurgling.

A bird nest, leaf debris or a frost cap on the vent opening can cause this. You can sometimes spot it from the ground, but climbing onto a roof in winter is not worth the risk. Describe the sound and when it happens, and a plumber can check the vent with a camera or a safe access point.

A narrowed line, not a plugged one

Grease, soap and starch cling to the walls of a drain pipe and build a thick lining. The pipe is not blocked, but it is half the size it should be. Water passes, only slowly, and every month the opening gets smaller.

A cable can poke a hole through that lining and leave the rest in place, which is why the sink is fast for a week and slow again after. Scouring the walls with hydro jetting removes the lining itself. For a single stubborn branch, ordinary drain cleaning is often enough.

A timing test you can run in two minutes

Fill the basin to the same level three times in a row and time how long each takes to empty. If all three runs take about the same time, the restriction is steady, which fits a narrowed pipe or a lined branch. If the first run is quick and later runs get slower, water is backing up behind something that fills as you go.

Next, run the cold tap on a trickle for a minute and watch the water level near the strainer. A level that creeps upward under a trickle means the pipe cannot carry even a small flow. That is worth telling the dispatcher, because it separates a nuisance from a line that will fail soon.

Also note whether the slowdown started suddenly or built over months. Sudden changes often follow something dropped or poured down the drain, while a slow decline usually means buildup that a cable alone will not remove.

What not to do

Skip the chemical drain cleaner. In a partly open pipe it sits in the trap, eats at seals and leaves caustic water for whoever opens the trap next. It also does little against a grease lining.

Do not keep running the disposal in the hope it will clear a slow line, and do not force a snake you do not know how to control. A cable can scratch the pipe or snag in a joint.
